# Title Card / Kinetic Typography

**What it is:** A piece where typography itself is the subject — the words are the visual, not a label on top of some other visual. A single statement animating on screen, a quote reveal, a chapter/section title.

**When to use this category:** The request is fundamentally about words appearing with intention — "animate this quote," "make a title card for this section," "I want this headline to feel alive." If there's a separate visual subject (a product, an icon, a chart) that the text supports, this is probably a different category (motion graphic or product demo) with text as a secondary element instead.

**Length:** 5–15s. Long enough for the words to be read comfortably at the pace they're revealed, short enough that it doesn't overstay a single idea.

## How to build it

1. **Decide the reading unit.** Is the whole statement revealed at once, word-by-word, or line-by-line? Pick one and commit — mixing reveal granularities within one card reads as indecisive. Word-by-word suits punchy, declarative statements; whole-line or whole-block suits a considered, quieter tone.
2. **Typography is doing all the work others categories give to a visual.** Scale, weight, and spacing changes substitute for the "visual subject" another category would have. A size jump on the one important word, or a weight change from Regular to Semibold on the key phrase, carries meaning the way an icon would elsewhere.
3. **Timing per word/line is not uniform.** A short, important word can hold longer than its length implies; connective words ("and", "but", "the") can move faster than content words. Vary the per-unit duration by the word's weight in the sentence, not by its length in characters.
4. **Avoid the reflexive failure mode named in Section 1 of the main skill**: letter-by-letter animation used just because it's available. Reserve letter-by-letter specifically for when a word itself is the payoff (a reveal, a punchline) — otherwise animate at the word or line level.
5. **Background stays genuinely secondary.** Since the text is the subject, the background should be close to inert — a static or very slowly shifting field, never competing motion. If the background needs its own motion to feel interesting, that's a sign the card needs a different category's visual subject instead.

**Pitfalls specific to this category:**
- Every word entering with the identical animation — defeats the whole point of typography-as-subject, which depends on variation to feel considered.
- Cramming a full paragraph in when the format wants one idea — if it needs bullet points or multiple ideas, split it into multiple cards.
- A decorative background shape "because it looked empty" — see the main skill's note on minimal-vs-flat: depth here should come from type scale and spacing, not an added shape.

**Transitions:** Between cards (not within one), prefer a hard cut or a quick push/slide over a cross-dissolve — type-led pieces read as more confident with a clean break between ideas than with an overlap that briefly shows two sets of text at once. See `../transitions.md`.

**Expected output bar:** Every word's timing feels deliberate on a second watch; no shape or color is competing with the text for attention.
